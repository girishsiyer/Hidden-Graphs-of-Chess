What is Graph Negative in Graph-Based Chess?

Graph Chess, or graph-based chess, is a new way of looking at chess positions. A position is divided into three fundamental graphs:

  1. Pawn Graph (PG)
  2. Mobility Graph (MG)
  3. Coordination Graph (CG)

The Pawn Graph (PG) represents the pawn chains in a chess position. It tells us how good the pawn structure is, or how good the map of the position looks.

Too many isolated pawns, doubled pawns, or other weaknesses can indicate a poor PG.

The Mobility Graph (MG) is related to each piece and its movement. It is not just about the path a piece can take from its starting position. It is also about its current location and the number of roles it plays in the position.

A piece that can play more useful roles has greater flexibility, and its contribution to the MG is better.

The Coordination Graph (CG) is the support network of pieces. It tells us how the pieces support each other. When I talk about the support system in chess, I am only talking about piece-to-piece coordination.

A pawn supporting a piece, or a piece guarding a pawn, is not part of the CG as defined here.

This does not mean that positional weaknesses are ignored. Weaknesses are considered separately.

Consider a position represented by the three graphs. The position contains positive graph structures, but it may also contain positional weaknesses. We do not discuss those weaknesses here.

The positive graph structure provides a priority list of moves. Prophylactic moves are considered first, followed by attacking moves.

However, the availability of an attacking move is not sufficient. We must calculate the consequences of the move and evaluate the resulting position.

This is where Graph Chess becomes interesting.

After calculation, Graph Chess considers questions such as:

  1. Did the piece's MG and flexibility improve?
  2. Did the move achieve an objective?
  3. Was the move a tactical operation?

The first question considers whether the moving piece has improved its role and flexibility. The second considers the purpose or objective of the move. The third recognizes that the move may simply be a tactic or sacrifice.

Therefore, Graph Chess does not need a separate initiative concept to cover these situations. The effects of the move are evaluated through the graph structures and through calculation.

Maintaining the Graph Structure

The main objective is to maintain a healthy graph structure after the move, unless the position demands a tactical operation.

The Pawn Graph should remain free from significant weaknesses.

The Mobility Graph should maintain or improve the flexibility of the pieces.

The Coordination Graph should remain properly structured. If a tactical operation temporarily disrupts the coordination structure, the structure should be rebuilt after the tactical sequence.

Therefore, what traditional chess describes as initiative can be viewed in Graph Chess as a consequence of the position's graph structure and the moves generated from it.

Graph Chess does not need to identify initiative as a separate advantage. It identifies the available moves, their objectives, their tactical consequences, and their effect on the graph structure.

The Book — Coming Soon

The three graphs provide a new way to look at a chess position. But understanding the graphs is only the beginning.

Hidden Graphs of Chess takes this idea further and develops it into a practical method for learning and playing chess.

The book explains how the three graphs can be used to understand a position, identify important strengths and weaknesses, generate candidate moves, and guide calculation. It also connects Graph Chess with traditional chess concepts, showing how both approaches can work together.

The book includes worked positions, graph analysis, examples, tactical ideas, and training positions so that the method can be studied and practiced step by step.

The aim is not to replace the chess knowledge you already have, but to give you another way to see the position — and another way to decide what deserves your attention.
