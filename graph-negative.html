What is Graph Negative in Graph-Based Chess?

This article is part of Graph Chess, an approach to describing and analyzing chess positions using relationships between pieces, pawns, and their possible roles. The ideas introduced here are individual concepts; their full connection and application are developed separately in the Graph Chess method.

Graph Chess, or graph-based chess, is a new way of looking at chess positions. A position is divided into three fundamental graphs:

  1. Pawn Graph (PG)
  2. Mobility Graph (MG)
  3. Coordination Graph (CG)

The Pawn Graph (PG) represents the pawn chains in a chess position. It tells us how good the pawn structure is, or how good the map of the position looks.

Too many isolated pawns, doubled pawns, or other weaknesses can indicate a poor PG.

The Mobility Graph (MG) is related to each piece and its movement. It is not just about the path a piece can take from its starting position. It is also about its current location and the number of roles it plays in the position.

A piece that can play more useful roles has greater flexibility, and its contribution to the MG is better.

The Coordination Graph (CG) is the support network of pieces. It tells us how the pieces support each other. When I talk about the support system in chess, I am only talking about piece-to-piece coordination.

A pawn supporting a piece, or a piece guarding a pawn, is not part of the CG as defined here.

You can read and learn more about Graph Chess in this article: What Is Graph Chess?

Graph Chess- A Positive Structure

When we look at a chess position, we think of three graph structures. We can also think of them as positive structures.

The PG represents the pawn chains. It provides a map of the position. Similarly, the MG represents the current activity and flexibility of the pieces. We wouldn’t want to place a piece on a passive square if we could place it somewhere more useful.

Graph moves are transformations of graphs. The idea is to maintain or improve the positive structure of the position. Similarly, when a chess piece supports other pieces, it contributes to the positive structure of the CG.

In short, Graph Chess looks at chess moves as transformations that should maintain or improve the structure of the graphs.

Do graphs have negative structures?

Yes!

Graph Negatives

In this article, I am going to introduce the negative structures of a position, known as Graph Negatives.

Traditionally, chess positions have weaknesses, and those weaknesses are exploited to beat the opponent. If you are familiar with positional chess, you will know that weaknesses can be related to:

  • King safety
  • Material
  • Pawn structure
  • Mobility and activity
  • Initiative
  • Development
  • Space advantage

Graph Chess also recognizes these elements and the weaknesses that arise from them. However, the graph model provides a filtering system. It helps us focus on specific weaknesses instead of treating every positional feature as equally important.

If we keep observing every possible weakness, we might get into time trouble or become confused by too many variables during calculation. There are already many candidate moves to consider during a game.

We need to calculate the possible outcomes of our candidates to select the best move. But selecting the right candidates in the first place can be difficult in traditional chess.

For higher-rated players, this might be less of a problem because they have learned thousands of chess patterns through experience.

The problem is more serious for improving players who are still trying to understand positions and decide what to do.

Here is the basic idea behind Graph Negatives:

A Graph Negative is a pawn or piece that no longer contributes properly to its respective graph.

A doubled or isolated pawn can be a negative in the PG because it loses some of the flexibility and structural benefits of a connected pawn chain.

A trapped or passive piece can be a negative in the MG because it has lost useful activity or flexibility.

A hanging piece is a negative in the CG because it has fallen outside the effective support network of the pieces.

In Graph Chess, these are our targets.

First, they are relatively easy to spot once you become familiar with the graph structures.

Second, they can help us decide what to attack instead of getting lost in the maze of positional features.

Of course, we still need to calculate whether an attack works. Identifying a negative is only the starting point.

Examples of Graph Negative

I am going to give three examples of Graph Negatives, one for each type of graph in a chess position.

Negatives of a pawn graph

When we look at a pawn structure, we find that pawns can lose flexibility in several ways. Pawns that cannot advance effectively, such as backward pawns, or pawns that have become structural weaknesses, such as doubled or isolated pawns, can be negatives in the PG.

If you spot them in your game and have an opportunity to attack them, you should consider doing so.

Here is an example of a negative in the Pawn Graph.

Figure: Bobby Fischer vs. Samuel Baron, 1956.

In this position, Fischer has just captured the bishop. Following the capture and recapture, Black is left with doubled pawns.

This is a negative in the Pawn Graph (PG). The doubled pawns have lost some of the structural benefits that a connected pawn chain can provide.

The idea is simple: identify the negative and look for an opportunity to exploit it.

Next, let’s look at an example of a Mobility Graph Negative.

Negatives of Mobility Graph (MG)

The Mobility Graph has two common kinds of negatives:

  • A piece with no useful role to play.
  • A trapped or passive piece.

No Roles for a Piece

A piece in a position is not very flexible if it has no useful role to play.

Such a piece may not attack anything, contribute meaningfully to the defence, or help control space in the position. That is why it can be considered a negative in the Mobility Graph (MG).

Trapped or a Passive Piece

Piece can be trapped if they are immovable, and do not have safe squares to go. If they have safe squares, they can still be doing nothing and remain passive. This is second kind of negative in the mobility graph (MG).

A piece can be trapped when it has no safe squares to move to. Even when it has safe squares available, it can still be passive if it isn’t doing anything useful.

This is the second kind of negative in the Mobility Graph.

The three graphs provide a new way to look at a chess position. But understanding the graphs is only the beginning.

Hidden Graphs of Chess takes this idea further and develops it into a practical method for learning and playing chess.

The book explains how the three graphs can be used to understand a position, identify important strengths and weaknesses, generate candidate moves, and guide calculation. It also connects Graph Chess with traditional chess concepts, showing how both approaches can work together.

The book includes worked positions, graph analysis, examples, tactical ideas, and training positions so that the method can be studied and practiced step by step.
