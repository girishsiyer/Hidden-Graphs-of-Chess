Hidden Graphs of Chess

A Graph-Based Method to Learn and Play Chess

What is Graph Chess?

Traditional chess uses tactics, positional concepts, and strategic planning to help players understand positions and choose moves. Graph Chess adds another layer of abstraction to this process.

Traditional chess is broadly classified into three areas:

  • Tactics
  • Positional Chess
  • Game Strategy

There are also many principles that guide players through different phases of a chess game.

Graph Chess looks at the same position from a different perspective. It represents relationships between pieces and pawns through three distinct graphs:

  • Pawn Graph (PG)
  • Mobility Graph (MG)
  • Coordination Graph (CG)

Traditional chess often examines positional features such as king safety, mobility, activity, and pawn structure as individual factors.

Graph Chess goes a step further by examining relationships between these features. This provides another way to understand a position and guide decision-making.

The Three Graphs

PG — Pawn Graph

A graph is a network of connected nodes. In chess, we can represent pieces and pawns as nodes and their relationships as connections.

Pawns naturally form structural relationships. One important example is a pawn chain.

The Pawn Graph (PG) represents positive structural relationships between pawns.

Isolated pawns, doubled pawns, and other structural weaknesses also affect the health of the pawn structure.

Another important aspect is flexibility. The PG can be evaluated using a scoring system that takes structural characteristics into account.

The details of the scoring system and its use in position analysis are developed in the book.

MG — Mobility Graph

The Mobility Graph (MG) examines a non-pawn piece in its current position. Rather than evaluating a piece by the path it followed to reach its square, we examine what it can do from where it stands now.

A piece can perform different roles, such as attacking, defending, supporting other pieces, or contributing to a plan.

Every piece except pawns has a Mobility Graph.

One important question is:

How many useful roles can the piece perform from its current position?

The more useful roles a piece can perform, the greater its flexibility. This flexibility can be represented through a scoring system.

Future positions are explored through analysis and calculation using graph-based methods.

Graph Chess does not replace traditional chess. You can switch between traditional and graph-based ways of looking at a position whenever needed. The two approaches are complementary rather than competitive.

CG — Coordination Graph

The Coordination Graph (CG) represents supporting relationships between pieces of the same side. These relationships can form a chain of supporting pieces.

A piece in the CG may support another piece while also having its own Mobility Graph (MG). This distinction is important.

In the following position, the knight on f3 supports the bishop on g5. The knight is also supported by the queen, creating a chain of supporting pieces.

The usefulness of the CG depends not only on the existence of supporting connections, but also on how effectively the pieces can maintain and use them.

An overloaded piece may have limited flexibility in this supporting network because it has too many responsibilities.

The CG can also be affected by tactical disruptions and sacrifices. When a connection is broken, coordination may need to be rebuilt or reorganized.

The detailed scoring and analysis of the CG are developed in the book.

The Book — Coming Soon

The three graphs provide a new way to look at a chess position. But understanding the graphs is only the beginning.

Hidden Graphs of Chess develops this idea into a practical method for learning and playing chess.

The book explains how the three graphs can help players understand positions, identify important strengths and weaknesses, generate candidate moves, and guide calculation. It also connects Graph Chess with traditional chess concepts, showing how both approaches can work together.

The book includes worked positions, graph analysis, examples, tactical ideas, and training positions so that the method can be studied and practised step by step.

The aim is not to replace the chess knowledge you already have, but to give you another way to see a position and decide what deserves your attention.
